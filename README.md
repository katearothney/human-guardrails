# Human Guardrails

I keep coming back to one question: what is AI doing to the parts of being human that I actually care about? Things like thinking a problem through, remembering, paying attention, and staying close to the people in my life. And if some of that is getting worse, what can we do about it?

This is me working that out in public. I'm not a neuroscientist and I don't have the answer. I read the research, I try to be straight about what it does and doesn't show, and mostly I care about the practical side: what actually helps.

I'm exploring two kinds of guardrails here.

The first has nothing to do with technology. It's the stuff that keeps us sharp on its own: alone time, moving your body, real conversations, being bored, getting rest.

The second is about the technology itself: limits and design choices we could build in so it treats us like people instead of usage numbers.

Both kinds live in [guardrails.md](guardrails.md).

## Why I care

We tend to pick up tools long before we understand what they do to us. AI is a strange case. Older tools mostly held our information for us. This one can do the actual thinking and remembering, and even some of the talking to other people. Sometimes that's a real gift. Other times I think it costs us something we won't notice until it's already gone. I want to be specific about which capacities are at risk, what the research really says, and what we can do.

## The capacities

I've grouped these three ways, because they're not the same kind of thing. Some happen inside your own head. Some are about what you're willing to believe. Some only show up in how you treat other people.

### In your own head

| Capacity | How AI can wear it down | One thing that helps | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[Effortful / critical thinking](capacities/effortful-thinking.md)** | You let it do the hard part before you've wrestled with it | Think it through first, then ask | Moderate |
| Memory and internalized knowledge | You remember where to find it, not the thing itself | Try to recall before you look it up | Mixed |
| Attention and focus | It gets harder to stay on one thing | Do one thing at a time | Contested |
| Creativity and originality | You make more, but everyone's ideas start to match | Draft before you prompt | Moderate |

### What you trust

| Capacity | How AI can wear it down | One thing that helps | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent judgment | You lean on its answer instead of your own | Make your own call first | Moderate |
| Telling what's real | You can't tell what a person made, and the label meant to help can make it worse | Check where it came from, especially when nothing is flagged | Emerging |
| Epistemic autonomy | It tells you what you want to hear | Ask it to disagree with you | Moderate |

### You and other people

| Capacity | How AI can wear it down | One thing that helps | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social connection | Asking it is easier than asking a person, so you stop asking the person | Ask a human the thing you'd rather ask a machine | Mixed |
| Emotional footing | It's always there and never pushes back, and you get attached faster than you notice | Notice when you'd rather tell it than tell someone | Emerging |

## What keeps showing up

The capacities are the list. The patterns are the part I find more interesting, because they hold across almost all of them.

- The version you like best is usually the version that costs you.
- The better the tool gets, the less you check it.
- A label that says something is safe can replace the act of checking whether it is.
- It happens fast, and nobody knows yet whether it comes back.

I've written these up properly in [patterns.md](patterns.md).

## Being honest about the evidence

The science on this is real but young, and a lot of it is messy. A few findings are solid. Most of it is small studies, surveys, or papers that haven't been peer reviewed, and some of the old "screens are rotting your brain" claims fell apart when people tried to repeat them. Used the right way, these tools can actually make us better at some of this. So I grade the evidence behind every claim, I keep in the studies that argue against my own worry, and I treat this as an open question I'm still figuring out.

Here's what the grades mean:

**Strong.** More than one study, at least one of them a proper randomized experiment, and it's held up when other people tried it.
**Moderate.** Consistent findings across a few studies, but mostly surveys or single experiments. No direct contradiction yet.
**Mixed.** Real studies pointing different directions, or an effect that shows up in some settings and not others.
**Emerging.** One study, or a preprint, or a claim that's reasoned rather than measured. Interesting, not settled.
**Contested.** Widely repeated, but the underlying evidence is weak or has failed to replicate. I'll say so.

Everything here is my opinion, not my employer's.

## How I look at each capacity

For each one I try to answer four things:

1. What it is, and why it's worth protecting.
2. How AI can wear it down.
3. What the evidence actually says, graded honestly, including the studies that disagree.
4. The guardrails, which is the part I care about most:
   - Beyond technology: things you do in your own life to stay strong, like alone time, activity, and real connection.
   - Built into technology: limits and design choices that make the tools safer and more human.
