# Effortful / critical thinking

## The capacity
The ability to sit with a hard problem, hold it in your head, and work it through: reasoning, weighing, doubting, and arriving at a view that is actually yours. It is slow and uncomfortable by design. A lot of learning depends on that discomfort. Psychologists call it "desirable difficulty": the struggle is not a bug in thinking, it is where the thinking happens.

## How AI can disrupt it
AI is very good at removing the struggle. Ask, and a fluent, confident answer appears before you have done any of the work. Three mechanisms:

- **Cognitive offloading.** We reliably hand mental work to tools when it lowers effort. Often that is smart. But when the work being offloaded *is* the thinking, we can skip the part that builds the skill. (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)
- **Cognitive debt.** Producing with an assistant can feel like thinking while engaging less. In one EEG study, people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker brain connectivity, remembered their own text less well, and felt less ownership of it. (Kosmyna et al., 2025, preprint)
- **Skipping the difficulty.** When a good-enough answer is free, the desirable difficulty that drives real learning never happens.

## What the evidence says
Honestly: this is an emerging, mixed picture, and most of it cannot yet show that AI *causes* a lasting decline in thinking.

- A 2025 survey found heavier AI-tool use correlated with more offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, with younger users most affected. But it is cross-sectional and self-reported, so it cannot separate "AI weakens thinking" from "people already less inclined to deep thinking offload more." The paper later received a formal correction. (Gerlich, 2025) — *preliminary*
- The EEG "cognitive debt" study is a small, non-peer-reviewed preprint, and its own authors warn against headlines like "ChatGPT makes you dumb." The effects are short-term and task-specific. (Kosmyna et al., 2025) — *preliminary*
- The underlying offloading mechanism is well established, but offloading is often *adaptive*: it frees attention for other work and is not inherently harmful. (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) — *moderate*

- Better evidence is on the way. A registered RCT protocol will use eye-tracking and fNIRS to measure cognitive effort during AI-assisted writing in 160 students (Chen et al., 2025), and a separate RCT already found students did better with an AI tutor but *worse* once it was removed (Bastani et al., 2024). The field is moving from surveys and preprints toward rigorous causal designs, but definitive results are not in yet.

**The honest summary:** the concern is plausible and worth taking seriously, the mechanisms are real, and the direct evidence is still thin. Used differently, the same tools can deepen thinking, for example a tireless sparring partner that asks you harder questions than you would ask yourself. *How* you use it may matter more than *whether* you use it.

## Human guardrails

**Personal practices**
- Generate *after* you have struggled, not before. Form your own view first, then let the model challenge it.
- Use AI to ask you questions, not just to hand you answers ("what am I missing?", "argue the other side").
- Write the first draft yourself. Bring AI in to critique, not to create.
- Retrieve from your own memory before you retrieve from the model.

**Product and design patterns**
- Build "productive struggle" in by default: coach and question before completing.
- Show the reasoning, not just the answer, so the user stays in the loop.
- Make offloading visible and optional, not the frictionless default.
- Offer a "tutor mode" that withholds the answer and walks you toward it.

**Policy and institutions**
- Redesign assessment so it measures thinking, not just output.
- Set norms that protect skill-building for novices, who have the most to lose.
- In procurement, ask vendors for evidence that a tool augments capability rather than replacing it.

## Open questions
- Does heavy AI use cause lasting change, or do effects disappear once the tool is removed?
- Who is most at risk: novices building skills, or experts who can safely offload?
- Which specific uses build thinking and which erode it, and can we design deliberately for the first?
