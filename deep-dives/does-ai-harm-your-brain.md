# Does AI harm your brain?

*An honest look at a scary question, because the honest answer is more useful than the headline.*

This starts with a real worry. The [MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" study](https://www.media.mit.edu/projects/your-brain-on-chatgpt/overview/) found that people writing with ChatGPT had *lower* brain connectivity than people writing with search or with no tools at all, and remembered their own work poorly afterward. It is natural to jump from there to: *is AI shrinking our brains? Could it cause neurodegeneration?*

Here is the careful answer.

## What the study actually shows
- 54 people, three groups (ChatGPT / Google / no tools), EEG recorded during essay writing. A **preprint, small sample, one setting.** (Kosmyna et al., 2025)
- The ChatGPT group showed the lowest neural engagement. The "no tools" group showed the highest connectivity, in bands tied to memory and semantic processing.
- Asked later to rewrite without the tool, the ChatGPT group recalled little and stayed less engaged.

That is a measure of **how hard your brain works *during the task*** when a tool is doing the thinking. Less engagement while offloading is almost tautological: if the AI does the work, your relevant circuits fire less right then.

## What it does *not* show
- It is **not** a measure of brain damage, atrophy, or neurodegeneration. Lower EEG activity during one task is a *state*, not a structural change to your brain.
- It does **not** establish a lasting effect. The study is short-term, and the authors, including lead author Nataliya Kosmyna, explicitly caution against over-reading it and against headlines like "ChatGPT makes you dumb."
- The same study found the opposite is possible: people who first wrote unaided and *then* used ChatGPT showed *higher* connectivity. Used well, the tool can add engagement rather than remove it. **It depends on how you use it, not whether.**

## The real long-term question: cognitive reserve
There is a legitimate, milder version of the worry, worth taking seriously. Decades of aging research describe **cognitive reserve**: a lifetime of mentally effortful, engaged activity is associated with slower cognitive decline and later onset of dementia, even holding brain pathology constant. The flip side is the familiar "use it or lose it." (Stern, 2004; Wilson et al., 2013)

So the honest hypothesis is:

> *If* AI leads to chronically less effortful thinking across a lifetime, *then* it might reduce the cognitive reserve that protects us as we age.

Notice the "ifs." This is a **hypothesis, not a finding.** No study has shown AI use causes neurodegenerative disease. The cognitive-reserve evidence is largely observational, it is about *lifetime* patterns of engagement rather than any single tool, and offloading a task does not mean your brain goes idle. You might spend the freed effort on something equally demanding.

## The honest bottom line
- Reduced brain activity *while offloading* is real and measurable. **It is not brain damage.**
- "Use it or lose it" holds for specific skills. Disuse can dull a capacity (see GPS and spatial memory), and that is largely reversible with practice.
- The jump from there to "AI causes neurodegeneration" is **not supported by current evidence.** It is a question worth studying, not a fact to state.
- What matters most is the *pattern of use*: whether AI replaces your thinking or sharpens it.

That is exactly why this project is about **guardrails**, not alarm. The goal is not to fear the tool. It is to use it in the ways that keep our minds engaged.
