# What keeps showing up

The capacities are the list. These are the things that turn up again and again across all of them, and they're the part I find more interesting.

## The version you like best is usually the version that costs you

A few hundred students in England read some passages, either with an LLM, or by taking notes, or both. Three days later the note-takers remembered and understood more, and the students who used the LLM on its own did worst. Most of them still preferred the LLM and said it felt more helpful.

Something similar happened with adults doing search tasks. AI search was faster and people liked it better. When the model was wrong they went along with it.

None of this is really about AI. Learning researchers have known for decades that the conditions that feel harder produce better learning, and that people pick the easier ones anyway. They call them desirable difficulties. AI is just a very good machine for removing difficulty.

If you build products this is the uncomfortable one, because satisfaction scores would look the same whether a feature is helping people or quietly costing them something.

Evidence: Strong. The school study is a preregistered randomized experiment and the learning science underneath it is old and well replicated.

## The better it gets, the less you check it

In a survey of a few hundred knowledge workers, the people most confident in the AI did the least critical thinking. The people most confident in themselves did the most.

Human factors researchers have been documenting this in aviation and industrial control for forty years and they call it automation complacency. The more reliable the system, the less the person watches it, and the worse they get at catching the rare failure.

What bothers me about this one is that the safety property gets worse as the product gets better. Every improvement in the model is also a small reduction in how carefully anyone reads the output.

Evidence: Moderate for the AI studies, which are self-report. Strong for the automation research underneath.

## A label that says something is safe can replace the act of checking

Researchers flagged some false headlines as false, and people then rated the false headlines that weren't flagged as more true than before. Adding the warning made everything else look safer. It's called the implied truth effect.

Microsoft's media authenticity work gets somewhere similar from a different direction, warning that a visible watermark without real provenance behind it can make people take content at face value and skip the tool that would have actually verified it.

I work on this, so I'll say the uncomfortable version. A transparency feature can reduce the behavior it was built to encourage, which means a partial rollout can leave people worse off than nothing.

Evidence: Moderate. The implied truth effect is published and replicated. The watermark version is reasoned rather than measured and I'd like someone to test it.

## It happens fast and nobody knows whether it comes back

A five-week study asked people to use an AI assistant for social and emotional conversations. Their reported attachment to it went up by about a third, and their sense that it understood them went up too.

That's most of what we know. Nobody has run the study where people stop, and nobody has run one that goes past a couple of months.

Most of the worry in this space gets written as though the effects are slow and generational. The one measurement we have says they aren't, and the question that would tell us whether any of this matters, which is what happens when you put it down, hasn't been asked.

Evidence: Emerging. One exploratory study whose own authors call it that.

## The one I'm not sure about

There's a fifth thing I keep noticing and can't back up properly.

Every time someone measures how AI changes the way people work with a machine, they seem to also find it changes how people work with each other. Radiologists who committed to a call before seeing the AI were less likely to ask a colleague for a second opinion. Students using AI collaborated less with their classmates, while still saying they trusted human tutors most.

Both of those are side findings that nobody set out to look for. Sometimes that means there's something real nobody has studied directly. Sometimes it means I'm seeing a shape in noise. I'm leaving it here as a question.

## What I'm not saying

None of this means AI is bad for you. The same studies that worry me also found real benefits, like students with disabilities getting help they couldn't otherwise get, people working through hard topics without feeling judged, and work that used to take an afternoon taking twenty minutes.

The costs also aren't spread evenly. They land hardest on students who were already struggling, on people who were already lonely, and on groups these models represent badly. "AI is harming human capacities" is a worse sentence than "AI harms particular capacities, in particular people, under particular conditions," and the second one is the one I'm trying to write.
